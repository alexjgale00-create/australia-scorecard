The current account balance measures whether a country is, on net, lending to or borrowing from the rest of the world. Australia has run current account deficits for most of the post-war era, financed by foreign investment — equity-heavy through the 1960s–80s, shifting toward debt after that, with mining and resources investment becoming the dominant driver only in more recent decades (RBA) — a contrast with Germany and South Korea, which ran current account surpluses of roughly 4–6% and 2–7% of GDP respectively over 2023–2025. Persistent deficits mean a growing stock of foreign-owned assets and debt, which can matter a great deal during a global credit squeeze. This gauge's "which direction is better" call is genuinely contestable — see the Methodology page — and is flagged there for review rather than asserted as settled.
